Sundai Hack #133 · Autolab × Maritime

A language model that grows
while it learns.

We let a model add parameters during training instead of building it at full size on day one — and then handed the search for the best growth schedule to an autonomous research agent.

github.com/qsimeon/growlab

The motivation

Every model you have ever trained was born at its final size.

Scaling laws answer a precise question: for a fixed compute budget, how many parameters should a model have, and how much data should it see? Those answers reshaped how the field spends money.

But they quietly assume the parameter count stays constant for the entire run. That assumption was never argued for. It is simply how models happen to be built.

If a model can change size while it trains, the real question is not how big to make it. It is what shape its growth should trace over time.

The idea

Training cost is the area under the parameter curve.

Compute per step scales with parameter count, so the total bill is the area beneath this curve. Early in training a model is learning cheap regularities — word frequencies, basic grammar — that need very little capacity. Paying for a large model from step zero means buying capacity long before there is anything to use it for.

What growing actually looks like

The model gains capacity in discrete steps, alternating between getting wider and getting deeper.

Each event copies the learned weights into a larger architecture and initialises the new dimensions so that gradients reach them immediately. The model absorbs each expansion within about twenty steps and carries on learning.

By the halfway point it has reached full size, and it finishes training there. Both the growing model and a conventional one end up with exactly the same architecture — 1.2 million parameters growing to 5.9 million.

The experiment

We changed exactly one thing.

To find out whether the shape of growth genuinely matters, we ran two groups of models that were identical in every respect except one. Both received the same learning-rate schedule, the same data in the same order, the same final architecture, and — critically — the same total compute budget, measured in floating-point operations rather than wall-clock time.

Being small early buys nineteen per cent more training steps for the same money. That is the entire mechanism we set out to test. We ran three independent models in each group so that we could tell a real effect apart from random variation.

Result

The growing model is ahead from the very beginning, and never gives the lead back.

It reaches the conventional model's final quality using roughly 60 per cent of the compute, then keeps improving with what is left over.

At the end of the budget the growing models scored 4.11 against 5.02 for the conventional ones.

Result

Every growing model beat every conventional one.

The three growing models landed within a few hundredths of each other. The three conventional models were scattered across a full point of loss.

Growth did not only produce a better model. It produced a far more predictable one.

Spread across repeated runs fell from 0.53 to 0.05. Starting small appears to protect the model during the fragile early phase, when an aggressive learning rate can otherwise destabilise it. Growth is behaving like a curriculum, not merely a saving.

The system

Two agents with two very different jobs.

The search space is far too large to explore by hand, so we handed it to an autonomous research agent. It reproduced our own schedule exactly, then went looking for something better — and found it.

What the agent discovered

When a model grows matters far more than how finely it grows.

Growing too early and growing too late were both clearly worse. Splitting the same growth into more, smaller steps made no difference at all.

What mattered was finishing growth around a third of the way through training, and alternating between widening and deepening rather than doing all the widening first.

The agent then stopped on its own and reported that the remaining differences were smaller than the noise between runs — asking whether we wanted to spend more compute to resolve them.

Looking forward

What we would do next.

These are small models trained briefly, and we say so plainly. The result is a strong signal rather than a settled fact, and the obvious next move is to repeat it at a scale people actually train at, and on more varied data.

Let the model decide when to grow. Right now growth happens on a fixed timetable. It should instead be triggered when the model stops improving — a signal that its current capacity has been used up.

Let it choose width or depth for itself. The agent showed that alternating beats widening first. A model could test both and take whichever helps more at that moment.

Push it to a serious scale. If growth still wins on a model large enough to be useful, it changes what a training budget buys.
